Effect of Demographic structure on Housing prices - A municipality-level analysis in France from 2014 to 2024

Research Question: How does the demographic composition of a municipality affect local housing prices?

Understanding the determinants of housing prices has long been a central topic in urban economics. While demand-side factors such as income, interest rates, or credit conditions are often highlighted, demographic composition represents a less explored dimension. Housing demand is affected by demographic structure through household formation, age distribution, family size, and socioeconomic characteristics, which shape the type, size, and location of residences required by local populations. The objective of this project is therefore to study how changes in the demographic structure of French municipalities between 2014 and 2024 have influenced local housing prices.

France is a relevant case study for answering our research question because it displays a wide diversity of spatial and demographic profiles. The French real estate market has undergone significant changes over the past decade, including rising average prices, varying transaction volumes, and growing spatial inequalities. Studying the link between demographic trends and housing prices over a ten-year period can therefore shed light on the mechanisms through which population structure influences local housing markets.

To explore this question, the project combines several datasets. Housing indicators are available at the municipal level and on an annual basis for the period 2014-2024. Demographic data come from French censuses (2011, 2016 and 2022), which provide detailed information on age structure, household size, employment categories, and other population characteristics. Although census variables are only available for a few reference years, they still enable us to capture the significant changes that have occurred in the demographic composition during the period. Working with panel data allows us to track each municipality over time and to observe how changes in its demographic profile are linked to variations in housing prices. The econometric analysis will rely on fixed-effects models, which help isolate the effect of demographic variables by controlling for characteristics specific to each municipality that do not change over time (geography, local amenities, or long-run development patterns). Although causal inference is challenging due to potential reverse causality (high housing prices may attract or deter certain demographic groups), the panel structure helps reduce this concern by observing demographic changes before and after price movements. Additional strategies may also be considered.

Our project is structured as follows. First, we present a descriptive analysis of the main variables in our datasets, using graphical and spatial visualisations to highlight key trends in both housing prices and demographic composition. Then we develop an econometric framework in order to isolate the effect of demographic structure on real estate prices. Finally, we address potential identification challenges, such as reverse causality and omitted variable bias, and discuss how our empirical strategy attempts to mitigate them.

II - Descriptive analysis of the structure of the French real estate market

a. Average price per m² at the municipality level

Between 2016 and 2022, the maps reveal an almost global increase in average prices per square meter, particularly marked in and around large metropolitan areas (we can distinguish Paris, Lyon, Marseille, Bordeaux, Toulouse…). This trend is followed by a significant gap between urban and rural areas, reflecting the dynamics of metropolitanization and differentiated attractiveness. The spread of high price levels in cities such as Bordeaux, Annecy, and Nantes suggests that the rise in prices extends beyond the Paris region and reflects common macroeconomic determinants combined with heterogeneous local trends. Coastal municipalities also have high prices per square meter, which can be explained by several factors such as tourist appeal, quality of life, and the presence of many second homes. From an economic point of view, these maps already allow us to make the following assumptions: prices per square meter seem to be correlated with several factors, including urbanity, unemployment, and the attractiveness of different areas.
These maps allow us to easily distinguish between attractive areas of France, such as cities and coastal regions, and less attractive areas, known as the “diagonale du vide” (empty diagonal), which stretches from the northeast to the west of the country.

b. The evolution of the average price of real estate per square meter

This graph confirms the trend observed on the maps: average real estate prices per square meter rose sharply in France between the mid-2010s and the mid-2020s. In fact, prices remained fairly stable between 2014 and 2019, hovering around 1600€ per m², with a slight decline observed in 2018.

Between 2019 and 2023, the average price per square meter in France rose sharply from 1600€ to almost 2000€.
This trend probably reflects several factors: very favorable borrowing conditions for most of the period due to low interest rates, stronger demand for real estate after the Covid-19 crisis, and a possible move to more attractive regions.

A downward trend is observed since 2023. This recent slowdown could be linked to the change in monetary policy and rising interest rates, which have reduced households’ purchasing power in the real estate market. Overall, this graph highlights a change in the market regime, which has shifted from a relatively stable situation at the outset to a phase of significant price appreciation that appears to be highly sensitive to macroeconomic and financial shocks.

c. Distribution of houses vs. apartments by year

In the first graphical representation we observe that the volume of transactions increased between 2014 and 2016, suggesting a gradual recovery in the real estate market after the sovereign debt crisis in the eurozone (which peaked in 2010-2012). A significant decline was observed in 2017-2018, which could reflect regulatory changes, data coverage issues, or tighter credit conditions that temporarily reduced market activity.

From 2019, the number of transactions increased strongly, exceeding one million in 2021 and remaining at a high level in 2022. This expansion is consistent with the exceptionally low interest rates observed before 2022 and with the sharp increase in demand for housing around the Covid-19 crisis, when households sought larger or better-located homes.

After 2022, volumes decline sharply until 2023-2024, indicating a slowdown in the market. This slowdown reflects rising mortgage rates and inflation, which have eroded purchasing power and increased the cost of financing.

However, the composition chart shows that, despite these pronounced fluctuations in activity, the market structure has remained remarkably stable: apartments have consistently accounted for just over half of transactions, around 55-58%, and houses around 42-45%. Only minor variations can be observed from year to year, with no lasting shift in favor of one type of housing over another.

III - Descriptive analysis of the demography

a. Demographic indicators

To go further in the data analysis process, we calculate several indicators.

  • The upper socio-economic group rate: percentage of managers and higher intellectual professions among the population of 15+ years old.

  • The unemployment rate: the percentage of unemployed people among the active population (between 20 years old and 64 years old).

It should be noted that this ratio is approximate, as it does not take into account individuals aged 15 to 19. As we did not have the number of inhabitants for each age group in each municipality, we chose the age group closest to the working population.

  • The relocation rate: the percentage of people that initiate a transaction during the year while they were not resident in this municipality previously.

These indicators were selected to capture the demographic dimensions of local housing demand.

The upper socio-economic group rate, CSP+ captures long-term socio-economic structure and reflects income levels, human capital, and purchasing power which directly affect households’ ability to borrow and therefore to buy housing.

Unemployment reflects short-term economic conditions and the labor market conditions.

Mobility measures housing market dynamism.

b. CSP+ rate by municipalities

As well as in the graph where average m² price are mapped, we can easily distinguish the French metropolises (Paris, Marseille, Lyon, Nantes, Grenoble, Toulouse, Nice, Bordeaux, Lilles…) and the “diagonale du vide”. It therefore seems to highlight a positive correlation between average m² price and the CSP+ rate in municipalities. In other words, municipalities with higher CSP+ rate also have higher average m² price.

Indeed, the share of highly skilled workers is a proxy for the average income level. Municipalities with a large proportion of CSP+ tend to have a population with higher income in average and therefore a higher borrowing power. This leads to higher demand for housing and apartments and an increase in price.

A high CSP+ rate also implies the presence of skilled jobs and a dynamic economic fabric, which can therefore attract residents and increase the housing and apartments demand.

We can’t notice very well an evolution between 2016 and 2022. However, this is probably because of the scale.

IV - Pre-Regression Analysis: Explorating the Correlations

A - Descriptive analysis of housing prices and structural characteristics

a. Joint distribution of average price per m² and average surface area

1) By Departments

In this graph, each point represents a department at one precise year. The scatter plot shows a negative relationship, which means that transactions involving larger homes tend to be associated with lower prices per square meter, while smaller properties command higher unit values. The dense scatter plot between approximately 80 and 110 m² shows that most French sales fall within this range, with prices largely clustered around 1500€ to 3000€ per m².

The extreme observations in the upper left correspond to very high prices for relatively small areas, probably reflecting Paris and a few particularly sought-after urban areas where land scarcity and high demand drive up valuations. This trend shows that price heterogeneity is determined not only by location, but also by the composition of the housing stock. Areas with a high proportion of small apartments have higher prices per square meter, while areas where houses and spacious dwellings are the norm have lower unit prices. These data confirm the need to control for average size or type of dwelling in any econometric analysis of real estate prices.

2) By Communes

The figure highlights a negative relationship between average surface area and average price per square meter, which shows that municipalities where transactions involve larger dwellings tend to exhibit lower prices per m². This pattern is intuitive and reflects a mechanical composition effect where larger properties (often houses) are typically cheaper per m² than smaller dwellings.

b. Joint distribution of average price per m² and share of apartments

1) By Departments

In this graph, the proportion of apartments is used as a proxy of urban density (a high propotion of apartments is the sign of a urbanized area). The scatter plot reveals a positive and approximately linear relationship, showing that municipalities with a higher proportion of apartments tend to have higher prices per m², while areas dominated by low proportions of apartments have the lowest unit prices.

A large number of observations below 25% apartments show that most French municipalities remain relatively low in density, with prices generally below 2000€ per m². The points on the far right, close to 100%, correspond to very dense urban areas, most likely Paris, where land scarcity, amenities, and the concentration of skilled households generate significantly higher valuations.

The slope suggests that price heterogeneity is strongly linked to the structure of the housing stock: compact real estate markets benefit more from location advantages than spacious residential markets. Therefore, any econometric assessment of real estate prices should take into account the proportion of apartments or the composition of the housing stock in order to avoid attributing the effects of density to other variables such as unemployment or income alone.

2) By communes

This graph reveals a positive raw correlation between the share of apartments and prices per m²: more apartment-dense municipalities tend to have higher prices. This reflects a classic urban pattern, as apartment-dominated areas are typically denser, more central, and more expensive.

c. Joint distribution of average price per m² and number of transactions

1) By Department

Here we can observe a positive relationship showing that, on average, municipalities with higher transaction volumes tend to have higher prices per square meter. A very dense concentration of points below 10000 transactions and 2000€ per m² shows that the majority of French municipalities correspond to small and relatively inexpensive local markets.

The observations between 15000 and 30000 transactions correspond to large metropolitan or suburban areas characterized by high demand and many amenities. However, the cloud becomes increasingly heterogeneous as prices rise. Several high-priced municipalities have limited transaction volumes, which means that expensive markets are not necessarily the most active.

In this graph, we see the coexistence of two mechanisms: in dynamic urban areas, high activity could reflect intense demand that drives up prices, while in expensive locations, affordability constraints and supply rigidity may limit the number of sales.

2) By Commune

This figure shows a strong positive relationship between prices and the (log) number of transactions. Municipalities with more active markets tend to have higher prices per m², and the relationship appears monotonic.

V- Econometric analysis on prices

a. Implementing the baseline two-way fixed effect regression

We implement a two-way fixed effects (TWFE) panel regression at the municipal level for 2016 and 2022. The dependent variable is the logarithm of the average price per square meter. We express the dependent variable using the logarithm in order to interpret the coefficients (beta) as a percentage change in price. The main regressors reflect the local demographic and socioeconomic structure: proportion of executives (CSP+) (TauxCSPsup), unemployment rate (TauxChom), and residential mobility rate (TauxDem). We add real estate market controls: average surface area (SurfaceMoy), proportion of apartments (PropAppart, as a proxy of urban form/density) and market activity (ln_mut = log1p(Nb_mutations)). We include municipal fixed effects (absorbing local characteristics that are invariant over time, such as geography and amenities) and annual fixed effects (absorbing national shocks). Standard errors are pooled at the municipal level.

The logarithm of the number of transactions, used as a proxy of the market activity, is strongly and positively associated with prices. Indeed, the coefficient shows that a 1% increase in transactions is associated with an increase in prices of about 3.3%, suggesting that more active markets experience stronger price growth. Average dwelling size is negatively related to prices, consistent with a price-per-square-meter mechanical effect, which appears when municipalities where the average surface increases tend to display lower prices per m². A higher proportion of apartments is associated with lower prices, conditional on municipality fixed effects, suggesting that increases in density within a municipality are correlated with weaker price growth.

Demographic variables have smaller effects. The share of CSP+ is positive and weakly significant, indicating that municipalities experiencing an increase in high-income households tend to see slightly faster price growth. In contrast, unemployment and residential mobility rates are not statistically significant here. The low within R² (≈ 2.5%) indicates that only a small fraction of within-municipality price variation is explained by these observables, which is expected given the strong role of fixed effects in absorbing persistent heterogeneity.

We observe an inverted-sign dynamic between the scatter plot and the regression on the share of apartments. This can be explained by the fact that the graph captures between-municipality differences, while the regression captures within-municipality changes. Conditional on a municipality’s fixed characteristics, an increase in the apartment share over time is associated with lower price growth. This likely reflects densification dynamics (new apartment construction for example), which tend to expand supply and moderate prices.

Changes in unemployment are also positive and significant (≈ 0.00143), while mobility is weakly significant, around the 10% level.

The variation in average surface area and in the share of apartments are negative and significant, reinforcing the idea that changes in the composition of transactions have a significant impact on price per square meter. Finally, the increase in transaction activity is positively associated with price growth. The explanatory power of the model remains low (R² ≈ 2.5%), reinforcing the idea that most price dynamics reflect factors not directly captured by observable demographic changes, such as credit conditions, expectations, or national housing cycles.

VI - Conclusion

The objective of this project was to examine the relationship between socio-demographic characteristics of French municipalities and housing prices, as well as how these relationships evolved between 2016 and 2022. Exploring municipality-level data from population censuses and real estate transaction databases, the analysis highlights the relationships between local socio-economic structures and average housing prices.

The results show that municipalities with a higher share of higher-skilled occupations tend to exhibit higher prices per square meter, all else equal. On the other hand, higher unemployment rates are generally associated with lower housing prices. Indicators related to residential mobility are also positively correlated with prices, suggesting that more attractive and dynamic areas face stronger housing demand. These findings are consistent with economic intuition: housing prices reflect expected income levels, labor-market conditions, and the overall attractiveness of locations.

However, we were unable to establish a significant relationship between the unemployment rate and the average price per square metre of property, or between the mobility rate and the average price per square metre for properties. Graphical and cartographic analyses complement the econometric analysis by illustrating that the relationship between demographic variables and housing prices is neither uniform nor strictly linear across space.

In addition, even if the two-way fixed effects specification controls for time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity across municipalities (such as persistent differences in climate or geography) and for common time shocks, our analysis remains incomplete as it does not capture unobserved factors that may vary over time inside municipalities, such as changes in local amenities, improvements in transport infrastructure or access to employment centers, or tourism development.

VII - Discussion and limits

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the econometric model relies on only two years of observation (2016 and 2022), corresponding to census years. This constraint limits the use of richer panel-data methods and prevents a detailed analysis of price dynamics over time. Access to annual and harmonized data would allow for more robust panel estimations, better control for municipality fixed effects, and a clearer identification of structural changes in housing markets.

The analysis focuses mainly on socio-professional categories, unemployment, and mobility indicators. Other demographic dimensions could have been explored in greater depth, such as age structure (e.g. student cities, ageing populations), the presence of coastal or tourist areas with a high share of retirees and secondary residences, or gender composition. These factors are likely to shape local housing demand and could refine the analysis.

It is important to note that potential endogeneity issues arise. Demographic variables may be both determinants and outcomes of housing prices: for instance, high prices can attract higher-income households while simultaneously excluding others. As a result, estimated coefficients are interpreted as conditional correlations rather than strictly causal effects. Future research could address this issue using more advanced identification strategies, such as instrumental variables or quasi-experimental approaches.

Finally, the measurement of housing markets itself is imperfect. Average prices per square meter aggregate highly heterogeneous properties and may generate substantial noise, especially in small municipalities with few transactions. In addition, the lack of detailed information on housing characteristics and local amenities limits the precision of the estimates.
